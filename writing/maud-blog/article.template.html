The study asked whether a local fine-tune could improve a specific contract-reading task. Each answer was checked against an expert label. I fixed the evaluation protocol before training, selected the checkpoint on validation, and then ran the final test.

Hardware and runtime

I used an RTX 3090 with 24 GB of VRAM and 4-bit QLoRA to fine-tune Qwen3-8B locally. The hardware configuration, throughput and recovery checks were recorded with the experiment.

Measured throughput: about 26.6 seconds per optimizer step. With validation included, this projected to roughly 12–13 hours per epoch under the recorded thermal limits.

Operational controls: after early temperature concerns, I capped GPU power at 200 W and CPU frequency at 3 GHz. A guard paused work for high temperatures or missing telemetry. A sensor failure triggered a protective pause; checkpoint recovery was verified before training resumed.

Dataset: MAUD merger-agreement questions

MAUD stands for Merger Agreement Understanding Dataset. It has expert labels for questions about M&A deal terms: closing conditions, material adverse effects, deal protection and more.

Why MAUD: its predefined questions and expert answer labels support direct, repeatable scoring. Its 92 parent questions become 144 question/subquestion tasks in our representation. The dataset supplies answer labels, so those were the training targets; no reasoning supervision was invented.

Hypothesis: fine-tuning on MAUD would improve answers on merger agreements kept out of fine-tuning.

Data audit: I independently confirmed the official counts—25,827 training, 6,753 validation and 6,651 test examples. Main-data rows from all 152 agreements appeared in every official split. Testing generalization to unseen agreements therefore required a separate split by source agreement.

Split policy: deterministic agreement-level partitions were frozen before model experiments. Abridged passages stayed with their source. I excluded 3,680 counterfactual examples with ambiguous source provenance and audited duplicates and label coverage.

Why accuracy was insufficient: choosing each question’s most common training answer achieved 81.13% test accuracy, with zero rare answers correct. I used equal-task macro-F1 as the primary metric to reduce the dominance of frequent answers.

Methods and research basis

I considered reproducing an original MAUD LegalBERT experiment first. I prioritized Qwen to address this study’s objective: measuring improvement in a local generative model. The LegalBERT reproduction remains separate work.

QLoRA and the training configuration

QLoRA keeps most of the base model’s large linear weights frozen in compact 4-bit storage. Small LoRA matrices learn adjustments alongside them. In this run, about 21.8 million adapter parameters were trainable.

The mixed data types have different jobs: NF4 saves storage, BF16 handles the quantized base’s calculations, and FP32 keeps the small trainable adapters at higher precision. The “8B” in the model name refers to its parameter count.

Each training example taught the model to produce an answer letter and an end token. Instructions and padding were masked from the loss. I used Qwen’s documented non-thinking format, then graded answers deterministically. No LLM judge was needed.

Context handling: complete prompt lengths were measured before selecting a 4,096-token context. Overflow raised an error. Both final evaluations had zero passage truncation.

Experiment 1: checkpoint selection

Result: epoch five had the highest validation macro-F1. All six normal epochs completed, with evaluation after each epoch. One epoch meant one natural-frequency pass through 25,225 training examples.

Selection rule: keep the checkpoint with the highest primary validation macro-F1. This rule was fixed before training.

Macro-F1 is computed across each task’s answer labels, then averaged equally across eligible tasks. Training-data coverage rules left 119 eligible tasks.

Interpretation: validation macro-F1 increased from 0.6149 at epoch one to 0.6547 at epoch five, then declined to 0.6514. The paired intervals for the later checkpoint differences included zero. Epoch five was selected under the predefined rule; repeated training would be needed to assess selection stability.

Accuracy, macro-F1 and rare-answer recall favored different checkpoints. Using a predefined primary metric kept selection consistent across those tradeoffs.

Experiment 2: rare-answer weighting

Hypothesis: increasing the loss weight of rare-target examples would improve rare-answer recall.

I restored the saved second epoch and trained an alternative third epoch with 3× loss weight on rare-target examples. The comparison was normal epoch three versus weighted epoch three: the same starting checkpoint and another pass through the data.

Final results on held-out agreements

I froze epoch five and its hashes before running the test comparison. Untuned and tuned Qwen used the same quantization, prompt, context policy and decoding. The test was kept out of prompt tuning and checkpoint selection.

  • Primary result: the selected adapter outperformed untuned Qwen and the training-majority baseline on macro-F1.
  • Output validity: invalid answers fell from six to zero.
  • Overlap sensitivity: removing 94 preflagged test rows left macro-F1 at 0.3668 for base Qwen and 0.6791 for the adapter.

Accuracy counts every example equally. Macro-F1 balances precision and recall across each task’s fixed answer labels, then gives eligible tasks equal weight. Undefined class F1 is zero. Rare recall is correctness on examples whose gold label occurs in at most 10% of that task’s training rows, including labels absent from training. Rare precision is correctness among the model’s rare-label predictions. Invalid answers count as wrong.

The intervals use 2,000 paired resamples of whole agreements, seed 42. All 119 eligible tasks occur in the full test; 722 bootstrap draws omit tasks absent from their sampled agreements, following the frozen rule. The intervals do not include variability from retraining with other seeds.

Rare answers: precision and recall

Recall decreased; precision increased. The base model answered 66 rare cases correctly and the adapter answered 57. Incorrect rare-label predictions fell from 1,300 to 47. These measure different aspects of performance.

Error analysis: rare targets represented 2.84% of training examples, and some labels had very little agreement-level support. Validation review also identified abridged source passages missing relevant context. These observations motivate further investigation into support and passage quality; they do not establish a single cause for the errors.

Next experiments

The following studies are proposed and remain unrun:

  1. Measure training variability. Repeat the selected recipe with at least three independent seeds. Agreement-level bootstrap intervals measure uncertainty across agreements; repeated training would measure sensitivity to initialization and data order.
  2. Test passage quality. Audit training passages under predefined rules, preserve original labels and document exclusions. Compare a controlled data variant on validation to test whether missing context contributes to rare-answer errors.
  3. Test hyperparameter interactions. Predefine a small joint search over learning rate, training duration and rare-example weight. Compare candidates on validation to test whether the weighting outcome depends on the training schedule.

An original LegalBERT/MAUD reproduction would also add a published reference point. That remains separate work. The current final test is now closed to tuning; another confirmatory claim would need previously unused agreements.

Scope and reproducibility

Supported conclusion: this fine-tune improved the predefined primary metric for passage-based MAUD questions on held-out agreements. The study used one training seed and 154 rare test cases. Full-contract retrieval, open-ended legal advice and general legal expertise were outside its scope.

MLflow kept the configurations, predictions and artifacts on premises. Final inference took about 52 minutes for base Qwen and 63 for the adapter, using roughly 11.52 and 11.60 GiB of peak allocated GPU memory. A larger untuned API comparison remains unrun.

  • Traceable inputs: pinned model and data revisions, frozen split manifests and recorded preprocessing.
  • Verifiable outputs: saved example-level predictions, deterministic scoring and independently reproduced metric calculations.
  • Recoverable training: adapters, optimizer, scheduler, RNG and trainer state saved in checkpoints, with resumption verified.
